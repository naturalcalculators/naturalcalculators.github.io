Fractions and exact answers

Why the answer comes back as a fraction, what to do when you wanted a decimal, and how simplified surds work.

Entering a fraction

Press the □/□ key and an empty stacked fraction appears with the cursor in the numerator. Type the top, press ▼ to drop to the bottom, type it, then ▶ to step out of the fraction entirely.

That last step matters. If you type 1, ▼, 2, and then +3 without pressing ▶, you have entered one over five, not a half plus three.

For a mixed number like two and three quarters, press SHIFT then □/□ for a three-box template.

Why you get a third instead of 0.3333333333

In textbook display mode the calculator gives the exact answer whenever it can express one. A third is exact. 0.3333333333 is a rounded-off approximation that happens to be ten digits long.

This is a feature, not the calculator being awkward. Exact values chain without accumulating error, and in most maths courses an exact answer is the one that earns the mark.

To get the decimal, press S⇔D. It toggles the answer between exact form and decimal form, as many times as you like, without recalculating anything.

If you would rather always see decimals, SHIFT MODE then 2 switches to line display, where answers come back as decimals by default.

Surds: why √8 becomes 2√2

The same principle applies to roots. √8 is exactly 2√2, because 8 = 4 × 2 and the square root of 4 comes out cleanly. The calculator pulls out every perfect-square factor it can find.

When you get a decimal anyway

If your input contains a decimal point, the answer comes back as a decimal — type 1.5 × 2 and you get 3, not a fraction. The calculator takes the decimal point as a signal about the form you are working in.

You will also get a decimal when no exact form exists. ln 2 is 0.6931471806 and there is no tidier way to write it.

Fraction arithmetic worth trying

  • Add unlike denominators — 1⁄3 + 1⁄7 gives 10⁄21, already in lowest terms.
  • Fractions inside roots — √(9⁄4) gives 3⁄2.
  • Negative powers — 4⁻¹ gives 1⁄4.
  • Factorials over factorials — 5! ÷ 2¹⁰ gives 15⁄128.
