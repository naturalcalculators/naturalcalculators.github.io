Logarithms: log, ln and any base

Two keys, two bases, and a third key for everything else. Plus what to do on a calculator that only has the first two.

log and ln are not the same key

  • log is the common logarithm, base 10. It answers “ten to what power gives this?” So log 1000 is 3.
  • ln is the natural logarithm, base e (about 2.71828). ln e is 1, and ln 1 is 0.

Both open a bracket for you, so log(1000 is enough — the closing bracket is optional at the end of a line.

Which one a question wants is almost always decided by the subject. Chemistry pH, decibels, the Richter scale and orders of magnitude use base 10. Anything involving growth, decay, half-lives or calculus uses ln, because it is the one whose derivative is tidy.

A logarithm to any base

The log□□ key gives you two boxes: the small one is the base, the large one is the number. Type the base, press ▶ to move across, type the number, then =.

So log base 2 of 8 is 2, ▶, 8, = — which gives 3, because 2³ = 8.

If your calculator has no any-base key

Use the change-of-base formula, which works on any calculator ever made:

logₖ(x) = log(x) ÷ log(b)

It works with ln just as well as log — the two cancel out — so log base 2 of 8 is ln 8 ÷ ln 2, which is again 3.

The inverse keys

A logarithm asks “what power?”. The inverse takes a power and gives the number back.

SHIFT log10■log
SHIFT lne■ln

These are how you finish most log problems. If you have solved down to ln x = 2.5, then x is e to the 2.5, so press SHIFT ln, type 2.5, and read off 12.18.

The three rules, on the keypad

Worth checking on the calculator once, because seeing them come out equal makes them stick:

  • log(ab) = log a + log b — try log 6 against log 2 + log 3.
  • log(a⁄b) = log a − log b.
  • log(a■) = n log a — try log 8 against 3 × log 2.

That last one is the useful one. It is what lets you pull an unknown down out of an exponent, which is the entire reason logarithms exist in a school syllabus.

When you get a Math ERROR

The logarithm of zero or of a negative number is undefined, so both return a Math ERROR. Not a bug — there is no power you can raise 10 to that gives −2, or 0.

If that happens mid-problem, it usually means a sign went missing earlier, or you took the log of a whole expression when you meant to take it of one term.

A worked example: half-life

A substance decays to 30% of its original amount. How many half-lives is that? You need 0.5■ = 0.3, so n = ln(0.3) ÷ ln(0.5).

Type ln(0.3) ÷ ln(0.5) and you get about 1.737 half-lives. Any base works here as long as you use the same one twice — log gives the identical answer.
